This is the first article in this section. In the future, I plan to use it mainly to record criticism and thoughts about game design, game experience, and industry phenomena. The arguments may not always be mature, and they may not always be pleasant, but I will try to make the problems clear.

Starting with Star Citizen feels a little conflicted. On one hand, it is still not a finished game. Much of its content remains in development, iteration, and experimentation, so judging it by the standards of a complete release may not be entirely fair. On the other hand, it is also a very unusual game. It has a unique appeal, enormous technical ambition, and many immersive experiences that other space games simply cannot provide. Precisely because it is both impressive and deeply flawed, it is worth discussing.

I think the biggest problem with Star Citizen right now is a sense of emptiness that runs through the entire play experience. That is the theme I want to discuss here. Over the two years I have played the game on and off, this feeling has appeared again and again: the game often makes me feel as if I am standing inside a huge, real, beautiful universe, while also making me wonder why I should stay there.

When you first enter Star Citizen, it really is striking. Your character wakes up in an apartment in a future city. You get up, leave the room, take an elevator, move through the city transit system, and arrive at the spaceport without a hard break. Then you call your ship, walk into the hangar, board it, start the engine, leave the atmosphere, and eventually fly toward an orbital station. The immersion of this process is extremely strong, and the experience is almost completely seamless. Compared with Starfield's frequent loading screens and repeated scene cuts, Star Citizen really does achieve a fantasy closer to living inside a space world.

But this is also where the problem begins.

After the tutorial ends, what often arrives is not freedom, but emptiness and confusion. You do not know what to do next. The game does not have the kind of long-term guiding quests common in traditional MMORPGs. It does not have a clear progression path, nor does it have a strong main structure that slowly brings the player into the world. What usually sits in front of the player is just a contract screen. Once you finish the tutorial, in theory, you are already a member of this universe.

The development team's vision is clearly to let players live in this world. Characters get thirsty and hungry. They can be affected by environments that are too cold or too hot. They can black out under high acceleration. These systems are not problems by themselves. In fact, they are an important source of Star Citizen's immersion. The issue is that life-simulation systems should not replace game content itself, and they should not become obstacles to understanding and exploring the game. A world can be complex, realistic, and less game-like, but it still needs to tell the player: what can I do now? Why should I do it? What new experience can I gain from doing it?

At its current stage, Star Citizen shows an extremely strong dependence on its community.

Want to collect a specific armor set or cosmetic outfit? Look up a guide.

Want to know where to buy a certain ship component? Look up a guide.

Want to know which locations can spawn a specific item? Look up a guide.

Want to finish a long mission and experience the truly cool scenes and content? Look up a guide.

Want to know how much the ore you found in a box is worth, what it is used for, and where to sell it? Still, look up a guide.

This shows how poorly the game integrates information internally. The in-game encyclopedia does not help much, and item descriptions are often vague. A large amount of basic information is not inside the game, but scattered across community wikis, player-built databases, route planners, trading websites, and tutorial videos. On the surface, this may seem to increase community participation. It does give many tool developers and veteran players a feeling that they are helping build the universe. But from the perspective of a new player, this design greatly raises the barrier to entry. It turns Star Citizen into a game that depends heavily on veteran guidance, community explanation, and external tools.

This is not hardcore design. It is a failure of information design.

Another disappointing area is its monetization model.

Players can spend around 45 dollars on a starter package to gain access to the game and a basic ship. As they play more, they can also continue spending money to buy or upgrade to larger ships, including torpedo ships, large transport ships, and even more extravagant vessels that cost hundreds or thousands of dollars. Strictly speaking, this system is not exactly the same as buying ships in the traditional sense. It is closer to a crowdfunding-style pledge: players provide financial support to the development team, and the team gives ships as rewards.

That narrative may have been understandable in the early days of the project, but after it continues for many years and develops into a stable rhythm of promotions, limited-time upgrades, and ship sales events, it becomes hard not to feel unhealthy. This is especially true when a game is still unfinished, yet has already built a huge consumer system around future content, future ships, and future gameplay. Players can easily fall into an awkward state: am I supporting a great game project, or am I paying for a future that is always delayed?

To be fair, Star Citizen has not yet reached the point of traditional pay-to-win stat selling. In theory, a solo player does not need an absurd amount of time to catch up to the average level of equipment and ships inside the game. If they are willing to invest the effort, roughly one hundred hours of play can potentially earn quite decent ships and equipment.

But the problem is that these things are not stable.

In the current version, many items, ships, and pieces of equipment bought with in-game currency may be reset at official launch or during a major version update. In other words, the time a player invests in the game cannot become long-term accumulation in the way it does in a traditional MMORPG. Of course, one can say this is normal for a testing phase, but for an ordinary player, it still weakens the sense of meaning. When I know that everything I worked for might be deleted, it becomes difficult to invest seriously in the same way I would in another MMO.

This further intensifies the game's emptiness: the world is huge, the process is realistic, but the goals are unstable.

Some people may say, "I do not play Star Citizen for numerical accumulation. I play it to experience the world. If there is a wipe, there is a wipe. I do not care." That view is valid. Star Citizen is indeed more suitable for experience-driven players than for players who only pursue levels, equipment, and progression.

Even so, it can still be disappointing.

Many of the game's most exciting pieces of content are not really prepared for new players or solo players. A large number of high-value missions and dungeons are clearly beyond what solo players can handle, and some areas exist in public space where PvP can erupt at any time. The result is that if you are not acting with a group, there are many places you may not even dare to approach. For solo players, the content that remains stable and playable is often limited to cargo hauling, mining, industry, low-difficulty mercenary missions, and a small number of dungeons that can be completed alone.

This creates a contradiction. On one hand, Star Citizen encourages you to immerse yourself in this universe. On the other hand, it often reminds you through content difficulty, opaque information, and high-risk public areas that you probably should not be playing alone.

The deeper problem appears in ship stats and multicrew ship design.

Star Citizen has a fundamental issue in its ship stat design: the development team does not seem to have fully handled diminishing marginal returns between player count and combat power. When four players each pilot a heavy fighter, they gain four complete sets of mobility, weapon systems, shields, armor, radar, missiles, and survivability redundancy. Enemies must lock, chase, and destroy four independent targets.

But if those four players enter the same multicrew ship, they share one hull, one set of key components, and one destruction risk. The turret firepower, repairs, engineering, or scanning capability brought by extra crew members often does not make up for the mobility, survivability, and tactical freedom they lose by giving up independent ships. The result is that multicrew ships struggle to compete with a fighter squad of the same player count in combat power per player.

This is not simply a matter of big ships being weak or small ships being strong. The problem is that the human-efficiency model for multicrew vehicles has not been established. A multicrew ship cannot be evaluated only by total combat power. It must be evaluated by how much actual combat value each additional player produces. If four people in four ships are more flexible, safer, more damaging, and more forgiving than four people crowded into one ship, then multicrew ships become an emotional choice rather than a rational one.

After the armor system is added, this problem may develop into an even tighter design deadlock.

To make large multicrew ships meaningful, the development team must give them sufficiently thick armor. Otherwise, they will be easily surrounded and destroyed by a few fighters, and they will not reflect the advantages of size, tonnage, and crew count at all. But if large ships have armor so thick that ordinary fighters can hardly deal effective damage, then small ships lose interaction space against large ships, and combat becomes simple numerical domination.

What makes this more troublesome is that even if large ships become more durable because of the armor system, that still does not solve the problem that spreading the same number of players across multiple ships may be more effective. Balance then gets trapped in a dilemma: if large ships are too fragile, nobody wants to crew the same ship together; if large ships are too tough, small ships cannot hurt them, and combat loses a sense of participation. The armor system should have made ship roles clearer, but without reasonable compensation in combat power per player, it may instead magnify the contradiction inside multicrew ship design.

So the problem with Star Citizen is not that it lacks content, nor that it lacks technical ability. Quite the opposite. It has many astonishing elements: a seamless universe, cities and space stations full of impressive detail, complex ship systems, powerful first-person immersion, and a fantasy of space life that few other games can provide.

The problem is that these astonishing parts have not been consistently transformed into meaningful game experience.

It has a world, but lacks clear goals.

It has systems, but lacks good enough information organization.

It has ships, but has not truly solved the human-efficiency problem of multicrew ships.

It has a long-term vision, but the time players invest right now often feels unstable.

It can make you feel as if you are truly standing inside a future universe, but it may not make you believe that your actions in that universe are meaningful.

That is what I mean by emptiness.

The most fascinating thing about Star Citizen is that it makes players believe a huge, complex, real space world will eventually arrive. Its most disappointing quality lies in the same place: before that future truly arrives, players often stand inside a world that is already magnificent, yet still incomplete in meaning, and repeatedly ask themselves one question: what exactly should I do next?
